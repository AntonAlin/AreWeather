Every number, and where it comes from

This page exists because a mountain forecast that you cannot check is just a confident-looking guess. Below is every data source with its licence, the terms this site operates under, and each formula it applies — including the ones that are approximations, labelled as such.

    1 · Sources and licences

    Every forecast value on this site originates from a national weather service and reaches your browser through Open-Meteo, which aggregates their open data. Nothing is scraped, and there is no intermediary server of mine in the path — your browser talks to Open-Meteo directly.

    3 · The pipeline

    Six raw model forecasts go in; one elevation-resolved forecast comes out. In order:

    1. Fetch — surface fields from six models at summit height, pressure-level fields from two, and an ensemble for spread.
    2. Weight — combine the six into one estimate using weights learned from measured error at this exact point.
    3. Correct — apply the learned temperature and wind bias correction at the anchor height, if it passed hold-out validation.
    4. Build the sounding — assemble a vertical profile from pressure-level data.
    5. Downscale — interpolate to every m band between valley and summit, anchored to the corrected surface estimate.
    6. Derive — wet bulb, phase, snow line, cloud base, wind chill, drift index.
    7. Score — turn all of it into two verdicts with named limiting factors.

    4 · Combining models

    Weighted mean

    Scalars are combined with a straight weighted mean over whichever models returned a value for that hour:

    x̄ = Σ(wᵢ · xᵢ) / Σwᵢ
    xᵢ
    model i's value for this hour
    wᵢ
    that model's skill weight (below); models with no value drop out of both sums

    Wind is a vector, not a number

    Averaging wind directions is a classic way to produce nonsense — the mean of 350° and 10° is 180°, exactly backwards. Wind is decomposed into components first:

    u = −v·sin(θ) v = −V·cos(θ) speed = √(ū² + v̄²) direction = atan2(−ū, −v̄)
    V, θ
    wind speed and the direction it blows from, in degrees
    u, v
    eastward and northward components, which average correctly

    Skill weights

    Each model's weight is a softmax over its measured mean absolute error, gently tilted by a prior that reflects native grid resolution:

    wᵢ ∝ pᵢ · exp( −(MAEᵢ − MAEbest) / τ ), τ = 0.7 °C
    MAEᵢ
    mean absolute temperature error of model i against ERA5-Land over the training window
    pᵢ
    prior, 0.90–1.35, higher for finer native grids
    τ
    temperature of the softmax: a model 0.7 °C worse than the best gets ~1/e of its weight
    Weights are renormalised each hour over the models that actually returned data.

    5 · The sounding, and why not a lapse rate

    The usual shortcut for mountain temperature is to subtract a fixed 0.65 °C per 100 m. That is right on average and badly wrong exactly when it matters. On a clear, calm winter night in Jämtland, cold air drains into the valley and the profile inverts: Åre village sits at −18 °C while the summit of Åreskutan is at −6 °C. A fixed lapse rate does not just miss this — it gets the sign backwards, by twelve degrees.

    So instead the app requests temperature, humidity, wind and geopotential height at pressure levels hPa from ICON and GFS, giving a genuine vertical profile above the mountain. Heights are averaged across whichever models answered; the profile is sorted by height and interpolated linearly:

    f(z) = fₖ₋₁ + (fₖ − fₖ₋₁) · (z − zₖ₋₁) / (zₖ − zₖ₋₁)
    z
    target elevation in metres
    k
    the first sounding level above z
    Outside the profile the gradient of the nearest layer is extended rather than clamped.

    Anchoring

    The sounding knows the shape of the atmosphere; the six-model surface ensemble knows the best value at summit height. Both are kept: the offset between them is applied in full at the anchor and decays with distance, so near the mountain you get the ensemble and far above it you get the free atmosphere.

    T(z) = Tsnd(z) + [ Tanchor − Tsnd(za) ] · exp( −|z − za| / L )
    Tsnd
    temperature interpolated from the sounding
    Tanchor
    skill-weighted, bias-corrected surface ensemble at anchor height
    za
    anchor elevation — the summit height passed to the API
    L
    m decay scale
    Relative humidity is anchored the same way and clamped to 1–100 %. If no sounding is available for an hour, the app falls back to a constant °C/100 m and says so on the profile card.

    Wind with height

    Near the ground the surface forecast wins — it knows about roughness and the boundary layer. Higher up, the free-atmosphere sounding takes over. Then terrain accelerates the flow towards an exposed summit, which is why a ridge feels twice as windy as the plateau below it.

    w = exp( −|z − za| / Lw ) u(z) = (1−w)·usnd(z) + w·usfc (same for v) E(z) = 1 + (Emax − 1) · [ (z − zbase) / (zsummit − zbase) ]1.4 V(z) = E(z) · √(u² + v²) gust = V(z) · G
    Lw
    m blend scale
    Emax
    per-peak terrain exposure factor, 1.18–1.40 (see the mountain table)
    G
    the surface model's own gust ratio, clamped to 1.15–2.30
    Approximation. The exposure factor is hand-set per summit from terrain shape, not measured or fitted. It is the least rigorous number on this site.

    Precipitation with height

    P(z) = Psfc · clamp( 1 + k · (z − za)/1000 , 0.55 , 1.9 )
    k
    per kilometre of ascent — forced ascent wrings more water out of the same air
    Approximation. A blunt linear stand-in for orographic enhancement, which really depends on wind direction relative to the slope and on airmass humidity.

    6 · Thermodynamics

    Dew point — Magnus-Tetens

    γ = ln(RH/100) + 17.625·T / (243.04 + T) Td = 243.04·γ / (17.625 − γ)
    T
    air temperature, °C
    RH
    relative humidity, %

    Wet-bulb temperature — Stull (2011)

    This is the variable that actually decides whether precipitation reaches you as snow. Air at +2 °C and 40 % humidity has a wet bulb near −1 °C, and it snows — which is why a forecast that places the snow line by air temperature puts it hundreds of metres too low.

    Tw = T·atan[0.151977·√(RH + 8.313659)] + atan(T + RH) − atan(RH − 1.676331) + 0.00391838·RH3/2·atan(0.023101·RH) − 4.686035
    Stull, R. (2011). Wet-Bulb Temperature from Relative Humidity and Air Temperature. J. Appl. Meteor. Climatol. 50, 2267–2269. Valid roughly −20 to +50 °C and 5–99 % RH.

    Precipitation phase and the snow line

    snow if Tw ≤ °C rain if Tw ≥ °C mix otherwise (liquid and solid split 50/50) snow line = elevation where Tw crosses °C, linearly interpolated between bands

    Snow ratio — millimetres of water to centimetres of snow

    Ten to one is the folk rule and it is wrong at both ends: warm snow packs dense, and the driest cold smoke sits near −12 °C. A step function of air temperature:

    depth (cm) = Psolid (mm) × ratio(T) / 10
    Approximation. Real snow density also depends on wind, riming and crystal habit, none of which are modelled here.

    Wind chill — Environment Canada / JAG-TI

    Twc = 13.12 + 0.6215·T − 11.37·V0.16 + 0.3965·T·V0.16
    V
    wind speed in km/h at 10 m
    Defined only for T ≤ 10 °C and V ≥ 4.8 km/h; outside that the air temperature is returned unchanged.

    Humidex, and one "feels like" number

    e = 6.11 · exp[ 5417.753 · (1/273.16 − 1/(273.15 + Td)) ] humidex = T + 0.5555·(e − 10) feels = Twc if T ≤ 10 °C feels = max(T, humidex) − clamp((Vm/s − 3)·0.25, 0, 4) if T > 10 °C
    The warm-side wind term is my own addition: 12 m/s on a ridge is worth a couple of degrees even in July. It is not part of the standard humidex.

    Cloud base — Espy's rule

    cloud base ≈ zvalley + 125 · (T − Td)
    Approximation. Strictly the lifting condensation level for a parcel from the valley floor. The summit is additionally reported as "in cloud" when its interpolated humidity exceeds 92 %.

    Wind loading index

    Not an avalanche forecast — only the meteorological half of the question: is there loose snow available, and is the wind strong enough to move it?

    available = clamp( newSnowcm / 8 , 0 , 1 ) cold = 1 if T < −1 °C, else clamp((0.5 − T)/1.5, 0.15, 1) transport = clamp( (V − ) / 9 , 0 , 1 )0.8 index = 100 · available · cold · transport
    newSnow
    last 6 hours in full, plus a quarter of the last 24
    V
    summit wind, m/s — m/s is roughly where dry loose snow starts moving
    Approximation, and a deliberately crude one. It ignores aspect, slope angle, terrain traps and the entire snowpack history — that is to say, most of what an avalanche forecast is about.

    7 · The machine learning

    All of it trains in your browser, on demand, from public data. Nothing is precomputed by me and nothing is sent anywhere.

    Training data

    days of archived forecasts from every model — what each one predicted at the time — against ERA5-Land reanalysis for the same hours and the same point. The window ends six days ago because the reanalysis lags.

    Standardisation

    zⱼ = (xⱼ − μⱼ) / σⱼ, with σⱼ = 1 where the feature is constant

    Ridge regression on the residual

    The target is not temperature — it is what the weighted ensemble got wrong. Solved in closed form by Gaussian elimination with partial pivoting; the intercept is not penalised.

    y = Ttruth − Tensemble β = (ZᵀZ + λnI)−1 Zᵀy, λ = 0.08 (temperature), 0.15 (wind) correction = clamp( βᵀz + ȳ , −6 , +6 ) °C
    Z
    standardised feature matrix, ~1000 rows × 9 columns
    λ
    ridge penalty — with this little data, a wide unregularised fit would memorise noise

    Features

    Nine, each with a physical reason to be there — a small model is the honest choice for a thousand rows:

    Precipitation probability — logistic regression

    p = 1 / (1 + e−(βᵀz + b)), target: ERA5-Land precipitation ≥ 0.1 mm
    features
    √(ensemble precipitation), fraction of models that are wet, humidity, cloud cover
    Fitted by batch gradient descent, 500 iterations, L2 penalty 0.01.

    Validation, and the right to switch itself off

    The last 25 % of the window is held out as a contiguous block — not a random split, which would leak information across neighbouring hours. Each learned component must beat the baseline it replaces on that block by at least 0.5 %, or it is disabled and the app tells you so on the learning-log card.

    MAE = mean |forecast − truth| (temperature, wind) Brier = mean (p − outcome)² (precipitation) use the correction ⟺ MAEcorrected < 0.995 · MAEbaseline
    The honest caveat about the target. ERA5-Land is a ~9 km reanalysis grid, not a summit weather station. It is a good reference for systematic model bias and a poor one for the last few hundred metres of local terrain effect. A correction trained this way makes the grid-scale forecast better; it cannot know that a particular gully funnels wind.

    Ensemble spread

    p10, p50, p90 = linearly interpolated quantiles over 20–40 ensemble members band at elevation z = quantile + [ T(z) − T(summit) ]
    The spread is computed at summit height and shifted to the displayed band, which assumes uncertainty is roughly constant through the depth of one mountain.

    8 · Activity scores

    Both scores start from a base and lose points to named penalties, so the interface can tell you what is wrong rather than only that something is. A ramp costs clamp((value − from) × slope, 0, cap) points; a flag costs a flat amount when its condition holds; a bonus adds points the same way. The result is clamped to 0–100.

    These tables are generated from the same configuration the scorer runs on — they cannot drift out of date.

    10 · What this is not

    This is not an avalanche forecast. The wind-loading index and new-snow figures are meteorological indices. They know nothing about aspect, slope angle, terrain traps, or the buried weak layer that decides whether a slope releases. For avalanche danger in the Swedish fjäll use lavinprognoser.se; for official warnings, SMHI.
    • Not a weather station. Every number is a model output, downscaled. No observation from the mountain itself enters the calculation.
    • Not validated against summit observations. The reported skill is against reanalysis, which is the best free reference available but not ground truth.
    • Coordinates and elevations are approximate summit positions, hand-entered.
    • Terrain exposure factors are hand-set, not fitted to anything.
    • The forecast degrades with range. Beyond about three days the ensemble spread on the hourly chart is more informative than the line through it.

    The source is public: every formula on this page is in js/physics.js, js/ml.js and js/forecast.js, and tools/selftest.mjs checks them against known cases offline.

    Email

    The address is not written anywhere in this page's source — only a ciphertext is. The key is derived from the answer below, so answering is what produces the address. It costs you five seconds and costs an address-harvesting crawler more than it is willing to spend.

    Proton Mail, so it is end-to-end encrypted if you write from Proton too. I answer when I am not on a mountain, which in winter can take a few days.

    If you are reporting a forecast that was wrong

    This is the useful kind of bug report, and the hardest to act on without detail. Include:

    • Which mountain, and the exact hour — the forecast changes every hour and I cannot reconstruct what you saw without it.
    • What the app said and what actually happened — "it said −4 °C and 8 m/s at the summit at 09:00, it was more like −12 °C and blowing hard".
    • Roughly when you loaded it, since a forecast made three days out is a different claim from one made that morning.
    • Whether the learning log said the correction was active or disabled.

    Systematic errors are the interesting ones. If a peak is always too warm, or the wind is always understated on one particular ridge, that is a fixable bug in the exposure factor or the downscaling — not just weather being weather.
